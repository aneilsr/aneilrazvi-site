Why I Care

Video that fits the person watching

Almost every tool built for autistic people and people with intellectual disabilities is designed as if its users were five years old. The good ones for teens and adults, like communication and scheduling apps, don’t do video. I wanted a player a teen or an adult could live with, set to how they see and hear.

Cubby started as a kids’ app. The caregivers I interviewed pointed me at teens and adults instead, and that pivot also walked me out of the COPPA minefield before I committed the real build. Then the caregivers testing it asked for control over the picture and the sound, and that became the center of the product. The player comes first: brightness, contrast, color, warmth, dim and a volume ceiling, set for each person. The caregiver-picked library, with no search, no recommendations and no autoplay, is built around it.

What I Did

Concept to App Store, owned end to end

  • Designed and shipped Cubby solo using AI-assisted tools, 0 to 1, from concept through design, compliance and launch: iPhone (App Store), the web, and Android in beta
  • Designed the core of the product, a player set for each person: brightness, contrast, color, warmth and dim, a volume ceiling, captions and speed, plus a one-tap sensory-safe preset
  • Repositioned from a saturated kids-video market to autistic teens and adults and people with intellectual and developmental disabilities, after competitive and legal analysis, removing COPPA exposure and validating with caregiver interviews
  • Built an accessibility-first design system with three sensory modes, Calm, Balanced and Engage, set in the Atkinson Hyperlegible typeface
  • Designed three roles (Admin, Curator, Viewer) so a capable adult can stock their own library without touching the account
  • Worked from Figma to a written build plan to AI-assisted tools in small batches, with every change checked on a preview before it ships

The Turn

Where it started, where it is now

The strongest decision on Cubby was a pivot: away from a crowded kids-video market, toward a community that actually needed what I was building.

Where it started

A kids-video concept in a crowded market

Cubby began as a parent-controlled kids’ viewing app: a Parent Hub, child profiles, and a curated feed of kid-safe videos. Then YouTube Kids shipped “Approved Content Only” for free, where a parent handpicks every video and search is off. That was my pitch, released by the incumbent, at no cost. Sensical and Kidoodle were free too. Every version of the kids product was now competing against a free, better-funded version of itself.

Where it is now

Video tuned for autistic teens and adults

The autism and intellectual disability app market is active and well funded, but nobody was doing caregiver-picked video for teens and adults. The segment’s own complaint is that everything is built for young children. My architecture, a library an adult they trust builds, was already that product with the age grown up. Caregiver interviews confirmed the direction, and I wrote the decision rule before I made the calls so I could not move the goalposts afterward. Then the caregivers moved the center of the product to the player itself.

The feature I deleted on purpose

Age tells you what to recommend and how to tune reading level. I built it, then took it out of the app and the database schema. Storing an age band is what manufactures the “actual knowledge” that re-triggers COPPA, so collecting it was the liability, not the protection.

What it cost: age-based personalization, and a feature I had already built.

Three roles, so adults run their own library

Parent and child is the easy model, and it is the wrong one for teens and adults. Curator is the middle tier: a capable adult stocks their own shelf without ever touching the account. “Nothing about us without us” is a core value here, and a product that treats a disabled adult like a child gets rejected by the advocates who recommend it.

What it cost: a more complex permission model than a parent and child split.

Why the design system had to go

Cubby had a finished 8-bit visual system. Sensory research for this population says avoid seven specific things, and the old system did all seven. Three per-profile modes replaced it: calm and balanced sit on light ground, engage inverts to a deep jewel ground with an amber accent for hyposensitive users.

What it cost: a complete design system, thrown away and rebuilt.

What I Got Wrong

The build was green and the product was broken

Three bugs in one batch. All three passed a clean build. All three passed a search of the shipped bundle. Every one was caught by opening the live app and reading what was actually on the screen.

Actually did

Any signed-in session satisfied it. /parent/* was reachable by URL.

filter set on
#youtube-player
Looked like

Accessibility filters apply to the video.

Actually did

The YouTube API replaces that element at runtime. Every visual setting did nothing.

:root { ... }
/* same specificity 0,1,0 */
/* later in the file */
Looked like

Three sensory modes, three palettes.

Actually did

The fallback won the cascade. The attribute flipped. No color followed.

Bundle presence is not behavior. A passing build proves nothing about correctness. Anything touching sessions, guards, roles, or design tokens now gets tested by driving production.

It still happens

In November 2026 a new pricing section shipped with Monthly and Yearly rows that looked clickable but weren’t. The build was green. I caught it by using the site, not by the build, and fixed it two days later. Yearly was never unavailable, since billing had its own Monthly and Yearly choice. The page just offered a choice it didn’t let you make.

Open questions

  • The discovery paradox. Total control also removes the happy accidents. One dad told me, “I don’t necessarily know how much I want to limit his discovery.” How do you add discovery back without the rabbit holes?
